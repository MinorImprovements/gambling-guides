How Texas Hold'em works

Everything the chart assumes you already know, in order.

The goal

You get two private cards — your [[hole cards]]. Five more are dealt face up in the middle, shared by everyone — the [[board]]. At the end, the best five-card hand made from any mix of your two and the five shared cards wins the pot. You can also just win by [[bet|betting]] until everyone else [[fold|folds]], which is how most hands actually end. Nobody ever has to show cards they didn't get paid for.

One hand, start to finish

  1. Blinds posted. Before any cards, the two players left of the [[button]] put money in: the [[sb|small blind]] and the [[bb|big blind]]. This is what makes the hand worth playing for — otherwise everyone could fold for free forever.
  2. Hole cards dealt — two to each player.
  3. [[preflop|Preflop]] betting. Starts with [[utg|UTG]], immediately left of the big blind, and moves [[clockwise]]. Each player [[fold|folds]], [[call|calls]], or [[raise|raises]]. This is the only round the chart covers.
  4. The [[flop]] — three shared cards at once. Betting starts with the small blind now, not UTG.
  5. The [[turn]] — a fourth shared card, then betting.
  6. The [[river]] — the fifth and last shared card, then the final betting round.
  7. [[showdown]]. If two or more players are still in, cards are turned over and the best five-card hand wins. See the Hand rankings tab for what beats what.

How much to put in at each of those betting rounds is its own subject — see the How to bet tab for sizing and the price calculator.

Then the button moves one seat clockwise and it all happens again — which is why position is a rotating turn, not a permanent advantage.

Why position decides so much

Acting later means acting with more information. From the [[button]] you have watched all seven other players make a decision before you have to make one; from [[utg|UTG]] you are guessing in the dark with seven players still to act behind you. Same cards, very different value. That is the whole reason one chart cannot answer "is this hand good" — the honest answer is "good where?"

So the ranges widen as you move around the table: about 11% of hands from UTG, about 44% from the button. Being last to act is worth roughly four times as many playable hands.

Reading a hand like "AKs"

Poker shorthand drops the suits, because only two things matter preflop: the two [[rank|ranks]], and whether they share a suit.

  • AA — a [[pocket pair]], two aces. No suit marker needed.
  • AKs — ace and king of the same suit ([[suited]]). Flushes come from here.
  • AKo — ace and king of different suits ([[offsuit]]). Same two ranks, meaningfully weaker.

That is exactly how the matrix is laid out: [[pocket pair|pairs]] run down the diagonal, [[suited]] hands sit above it, [[offsuit]] hands below it. Suited versions are up and to the right of their offsuit twins, which is why the top-right corner is the strong corner.

What the four colors mean

  • R — strong enough to [[open|open-raise]] from any seat, including the worst one.
  • O — playable once you reach middle position or later.
  • Y — [[lp|late position]] only: the [[co|cutoff]], the [[button]], and sometimes the blinds.
  • Fold (grey) — not worth opening from anywhere in this game. Most of the grid is grey, and that is correct. Folding most hands is the single biggest thing separating winning players from losing ones.

How much to raise

When you do open, make it about 2.5 to 3 big blinds. Add roughly one big blind for each [[limp|limper]] already in the pot. Raising a consistent amount regardless of your cards is what keeps your [[range]] hidden — a [[sizing tell]] gives observant opponents your hand for free. Full sizing guidance, preflop and after, is on the How to bet tab.

What this chart answers — and what it doesn't

It answers: nobody has raised yet, it is your turn, should you raise or fold? Pick your seat, find your hand. Colored means raise, grey means fold.

It does not answer: what to do [[facing a raise]] (whether to [[3bet|3-bet]], call, or fold), what to do when you get [[3bet|3-bet]] after raising, how to adjust when players have already [[limp|limped]] in, or anything at all once the [[flop]] comes down.

One wrinkle worth knowing: the [[bb|big blind]]'s 38% is a [[defend]] range, not an opening range — it mixes calling and raising. Read it as "don't fold these," and raise only the top of it.

The seats, in action order

Preflop the action runs [[utg|UTG]] → [[utg1|UTG+1]] → [[lj|lojack]] → [[hj|hijack]] → [[co|cutoff]] → [[button]] → [[sb|small blind]] → [[bb|big blind]]. The blinds act last before the flop and first after it — a bad trade, and the reason you need a premium hand to voluntarily put money in from there.

How to bet

What the actions are, how much to make it, and the three numbers that decide every close call.

Your five options, every turn

  • [[check|Check]] — pass, keeping your cards. Only available if nobody has bet yet this round.
  • [[bet|Bet]] — put chips in when nobody has yet.
  • [[call|Call]] — match the current bet exactly.
  • [[raise|Raise]] — increase it. The [[min-raise|minimum raise]] is the size of the last bet or raise, so if someone bets 5 you must make it at least 10.
  • [[fold|Fold]] — give up the hand and anything you already put in.

This is [[no-limit]] Hold'em, so the maximum is always everything in front of you — [[all-in]]. That is what makes sizing a real decision rather than a formality: you choose the price, and the price is the whole game.

The one idea underneath all sizing

Every bet you make is an offer of a price. Bet small and your opponent is being offered a bargain — they can continue with weak hands profitably. Bet big and the price is steep — they must fold more, but you risk more when you are wrong. So pick your size from what you want to happen:

  • [[value bet|Value]] — you expect to be called and to win. Size up to whatever they will still pay.
  • [[bluff|Bluff]] — you expect to win only if they fold. Size to the cheapest amount that still folds out the hands you need gone.
  • [[semi-bluff|Semi-bluff]] — you are likely behind now but have [[outs]]. Betting wins the pot immediately sometimes and improves later — the strongest reason to bet there is.

Notice what is not on that list: "because I don't know what to do." If a bet is neither getting called by worse nor folding out better, [[check|checking]] is the answer.

Postflop sizing — always a share of the pot

After the flop, forget big blinds and think in percent of the pot. The pot is what you are playing for, so it sets the scale.

The three numbers, in words

  • [[pot odds|Pot odds]] / [[equity]] needed. You must win more often than your share of the final pot. Facing a half-pot bet you need about 25%; facing a pot-sized bet, 33%. This is the only honest answer to "should I call?"
  • [[outs|Outs]] and the [[rule of 2 and 4]]. Count the cards that make your hand. On the flop, multiply by 4 for a rough chance of getting there by the river; on the turn, multiply by 2. Nine flush outs on the flop ≈ 36% — enough to call a half-pot bet, not enough for a pot-sized one.
  • Break-even [[bluff]] frequency. Your bet risks B to win the pot P, so it needs to work B/(P+B) of the time. A half-pot bluff needs folds one time in three; a pot-sized bluff, one time in two. Its mirror is [[mdf|minimum defense frequency]] — how much your opponent must continue with so you cannot profitably bet garbage.

A worked hand

Pot is 10bb on the turn. You have four cards to a flush and your opponent bets 5bb. You need 25% to call; nine flush outs with one card to come is about 18%. Calling loses money.

Now give yourself two [[overcard|overcards]] as well, so roughly 12 outs — about 24%. Still marginally short, but if you can win extra on the river when you hit, or take the pot away later, the call becomes fine. That extra is called implied odds, and it is why this is judgment rather than arithmetic.

Mistakes that cost the most

  • [[sizing tell|Sizing tells]]. Betting big with strong hands and small with weak ones is readable within one session. Use the same size for your whole range in a given spot.
  • Min-raising after the flop. It offers a wonderful price, folds nothing out, and builds a pot for the player with the better hand. Raise properly or don't raise.
  • Betting a hand that cannot call a raise. If you would have to fold to a raise, often [[check|checking]] is better — you keep the pot small with a hand that wants a small pot.
  • "I'm [[pot-committed|pot committed]]." Money already in the pot is not yours anymore. The only question is whether this call, at this price, wins often enough.
  • Chasing without the odds. The calculator above takes about four seconds. Use it until the common prices are memorized.

One honest caveat: every number here is the break-even point, not a decision. Break-even plus position, plus the chance they fold later, plus knowing the player — that is what makes it a call. The arithmetic just tells you how much the rest has to carry.

How to read this list

  • # is the rank out of 169 — AA at 1, 32o at the bottom.
  • Top % is how much of all 1,326 [[combos]] you have covered by that row. This is the column that ties the list to the table: UTG opens the top 11%, the [[button]] the top 44%. The blue cutoff lines mark exactly where each seat stops.
  • Earliest seat is the first seat in the betting order that can [[open]] the hand. "—" means it is not an opening hand from anywhere.
  • [[tier|Tier]] and [[chen|Chen]] are the two things that produced the order: tier first, Chen score to break the ties inside a tier.

Don't over-trust the exact number

Hand 38 and hand 44 are not meaningfully different, and no two published charts agree on that part of the list. What is solid is the grouping — premium, strong, speculative, junk — and the broad shape: pairs and big suited cards at the top, offsuit junk at the bottom, [[suited]] always above its [[offsuit]] twin.

What also moves hands around is the thing a static list cannot show: 76s is better than K8o in a pot with three callers and worse heads-up against a tight raiser. The list orders hands for the spot this tool is built for — opening an unopened pot — and nothing else.

Things that trip people up

  • Seven cards, best five. You are not stuck with your two — and you are not required to use them. If the board is the best five cards available, everyone still in has that same hand and the pot is split.
  • Suits never break ties. No suit outranks another. Identical hands split the pot.
  • [[kicker|Kickers]] decide close calls. Two players with one pair of kings: the higher next card wins. This is why AKo beats KQo far more often than it looks like it should.
  • An ace plays both ends for straights. A-2-3-4-5 is a straight (the "wheel") and so is T-J-Q-K-A, but you cannot wrap around — Q-K-A-2-3 is nothing.
  • A flush needs five of one suit, not four. This is why [[suited]] hands are better, not automatically good: you flop a flush about 1 time in 120.
