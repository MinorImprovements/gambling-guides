How blackjack works

Everything the chart assumes you already know, in order.

The goal

Get closer to 21 than the dealer without going over. That is the whole game, and the order of those two clauses matters: going over — [[bust|busting]] — loses immediately, before the dealer plays at all. Roughly 8% of every bet the house wins comes from players busting hands the dealer would have busted too.

You are not trying to make 21. You are trying to beat one specific opponent who has no choices to make.

Card values

Number cards are worth their number. Jack, queen and king are worth 10. An ace is worth 11 or 1, whichever helps — and it switches automatically as the hand develops. A hand with an ace still counting 11 is [[soft|soft]]; once the ace has to drop to 1, the hand is [[hard|hard]]. A-6 is a soft 17; A-6-9 is a hard 16.

One hand, start to finish

  1. You bet. Before any cards. Unlike poker, this is the last time you commit money without information — everything after is a response to what you see.
  2. Cards dealt. Two to you, face up. Two to the dealer: one face up — the [[upcard]] — and one face down, the [[hole card]]. That single visible card is the entire basis of every decision you will make.
  3. [[blackjack|Blackjack]] check. If you were dealt an ace plus a ten-value card, that is blackjack and it pays [[3:2]] immediately. If the dealer's upcard is an ace or a ten, they [[peek|peek]] at the hole card; if they have blackjack, the hand ends there.
  4. Your turn. [[hit|Hit]], [[stand]], [[double]], [[split]] or [[surrender]] — in that order of how often they come up. You can keep hitting as long as you like; bust and you are done.
  5. The dealer plays. They turn over the hole card and follow a fixed rule with no judgement in it: hit until 17 or more, then stop. That is all. The dealer cannot double, cannot split, and cannot decide to play differently because of what you have.
  6. Settle. Higher total wins. Equal totals [[push]] and you get your bet back. If the dealer busts, every hand still standing wins.

Your five options

  • [[hit|Hit]] — take another card. Repeatable.
  • [[stand|Stand]] — take nothing and pass to the dealer.
  • [[double|Double down]] — double your bet and take exactly one more card, then you are done. The most under-used winning play in the game: most players double on 11 and nothing else, and leave money on the table on 9, 10 and soft hands.
  • [[split|Split]] — with two cards of the same rank, separate them into two hands and match your original bet on the second. Each then plays independently.
  • [[surrender|Surrender]] — give up the hand and lose half your bet, available only as your first decision. Looks cowardly, is correct in exactly three or four spots, and is worth about 0.07% when used in only those spots.

Insurance is not a decision — it is a no. When the dealer shows an ace you will be offered [[insurance]], a side bet of half your stake that pays 2:1 if the dealer has blackjack. It wins about 30.9% of the time in a six-deck shoe and needs 33.3% to break even, so it loses money on every hand. "Even money" on your own blackjack is the same bet in a costume. The only exception is if you are [[counting|counting cards]] and the [[true count]] is +3 or higher, because then the deck is ten-rich enough to flip it.

Why the dealer's fixed rule is the whole game

The dealer must hit 16 and must stand on 17. That means their upcard tells you a lot about how often they will bust: a 5 or 6 showing busts about 42% of the time, while a 10 or an ace busts only about 21%. The chart's shape falls straight out of this. Against 4, 5 and 6 you stand on weak totals and let them break; against 9, 10 and ace you are forced to improve a hand that is probably losing.

This is also why the house wins at all. The dealer acts last, so when you both bust, you have already lost. Play perfect basic strategy at a decent six-deck game and the edge against you is around 0.5%. Play by instinct and it is 2–3%. That gap — four to six times your losses — is the only thing on this page that matters.

Three habits that cost real money

  • Standing on 16 against a 7. It feels safe. You win 16 vs 7 by standing about 30% of the time and by hitting about 41%. Hit it.
  • Not splitting 8s against a 9, 10 or ace. Hard 16 is the worst hand in blackjack. Splitting it turns one terrible hand into two mediocre ones, which loses less. Losing less is the play.
  • Taking insurance with a good hand. Your cards have nothing to do with it. It is a separate bet on the dealer's hole card, and it is a bad one.

What is not on this page. Progressive betting systems, "due" cards, mimicking the dealer, and never busting are all losing strategies with confident-sounding names. No betting pattern changes the odds of the next hand, because the cards do not know what you bet. The only two things that measurably move the house edge are playing the chart correctly and choosing a table with better rules.

Rules worth walking away from

  • Blackjack pays 6:5 instead of 3:2 — costs about 1.4%. This single rule can more than triple the house edge. Switch it on in the rules row and watch the session numbers.
  • Dealer hits soft 17 ([[h17|H17]]) — costs about 0.22%, and changes a handful of chart cells.
  • No double after split — about 0.14%.
  • Double restricted to 10–11 — about 0.2%.

Everything in that list is set on the Play tab under Rules, and the chart re-derives itself when you change it.

Card counting

What it actually is, what it is worth, and why basic strategy comes first.

The one idea

Counting does not predict the next card. It tracks whether the cards left in the [[shoe]] are richer in tens and aces than normal. Ten-rich shoes favour you, for three reasons that all point the same way: you get more blackjacks and they pay 3:2 while the dealer's pay 1:1; the dealer busts more often when forced to hit a stiff total; and your [[double|doubles]] land better.

Low cards favour the dealer. So you count them against each other.

Running count to true count

A running count of +6 means something very different with six decks left than with one. Divide to normalise — that gives the [[true count]]:

true count = running count ÷ decks remaining

True count is the number every decision below is keyed to. One rough conversion: each +1 of true count is worth about 0.5% back to you, so a basic-strategy game at −0.5% breaks even around true +1 and is genuinely in your favour from about +2 up.

The order of operations is not negotiable. Counting with sloppy basic strategy loses money faster than flat-betting with perfect basic strategy. The count is worth roughly 0.5–1.5% when executed well; a handful of chart errors per shoe costs more than that. Get the Drill score to 100% and keep it there before any of this is worth your attention.

Why it is hard in practice

  • Penetration. If the dealer shuffles with two decks left in a six-deck shoe, the true count rarely gets high enough to matter. Deep penetration is the single biggest factor in whether a game is beatable at all — the shoe meter on the Play tab shows how far in you are.
  • Bet spread. The edge only pays if you bet much more at high counts than low ones, and a large spread is exactly what gets noticed.
  • Variance. A 1% edge with blackjack's swings means losing sessions are routine over any number of hands a human actually plays. The arithmetic is sound and the short run says nothing about it.
  • Countermeasures. Continuous shuffling machines reduce the count's value to nothing. Casinos are private property and may ask anyone to leave.
